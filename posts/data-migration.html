Why Data Migrations Go Beyond Lift and Shift

May 2026 · 8 min read · Warehouses

Data migrations are among the most common responsibilities for data engineers, which sounded deceptively simple to me until I worked on my first migration between cloud data warehouses.

Changing Requirements

I think the biggest difference between the clean theortical narrative and real-world implementation comes from changing requirements.

In practice, regular business processes don't stand still in the midst of data migrations.

This necessarily means that while data is being migrated, the pipelines will likely also be changing. With evolving data transformations and even schema, validating migrated data becomes complicated. While some discrepancies are expected, determining the extent to which they are acceptable and not indicative of actual issues can be a time consuming process. Beyond validation, all downstream impacts of such changes need to be accounted for, adding further challenges.

Interconnected Data

This leads me to the second source of complexity, which comes from the interconnected nature of analytical databases. Tables rarely function independently; downstream models often rely on many upstream transformations and dependencies. Even a small schema change in one table can cascade across dashboards, derived tables, and reporting pipelines.

Without detailed communication and coordination between teams, debugging missing or discrepant data becomes incredibly complicated.

From finding the discrepancy to exmaining the migrated transformation script, to comparing data across all upstream tables, the time to debug errors in a single table can grow dramatically.

Data Validation

Data validation is a blog post in and of itself, but I would be remiss not to mention its impact on data migrations here. Between maintaining a source of truth and employing different validation processes on changing data, validation often ends up becoming a case-by-case process.

Simple row count checks are not sufficient - a migrated table may match its legacy counterpart's row count exactly while still containing missing values, duplicate records, incorrect joins, or subtly different transformation logic.

As a beginner, I would often find errors in my "validated" tables only after the data was visualized further downstream. This experience taught me that manual validation by itself was not a failsafe method of robust data validation.

Historical Backfills

Lastly, backfilling historical data has proven to be a point of complexity in my data migration experience. It is a time consuming and expensive process, particularly when data is being regenerated using updated transformations instead of copied directly from legacy tables.

One lesson I learned the hard was was regarding scheduling. When backfilling a table day by day to limit compute usage, I wrote a shell script to repeatedly execte a dbt model across a historical date range. I let the process run overnight, only to discover the next morning that one failed run had halted the entire backfill shortly after I stopped monitoring it. After this surprise, I started building Airflow DAGs even for one-time backfills to ensure that retries, logging, and monitoring could be handled automatically.

Of course, data migrations involve many other nitty-gritty details such as SQL syntax differences between platforms and parsing nested JSON columns. However, those problems are generally more deterministic than the high-level operational challenges discussed in this article.
